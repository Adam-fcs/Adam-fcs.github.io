Abstract
Rapid climate change is transforming the Arctic Ocean through complex interactions
among the ocean, atmosphere, and sea ice. Understanding the physical processes governing
the Arctic Ocean changes is essential to examining its broader impacts. This thesis
investigates oceanic processes and their coupling with the atmosphere and sea ice in the
polar and subpolar oceans. The research integrates numerical simulations using the
Nucleus for European Modelling of the Ocean (NEMO) at multiple configurations
(regional and global) and resolutions (1/4 ° , 1/12 ° , and 1/60 ° ) with satellite and
hydrographic observations to examine key mechanisms shaping high-latitude oceans
variability. First, I study the properties and dynamics of large mesoscale eddies in the
Labrador Sea from a traditional satellite altimetry product. Combined with an
observationally-based hydrographic dataset, I examine the vertical heat flux from a long-
lasting cyclone and its impact on melting sea ice on the western shelf. Second, I explore
changes in the seasonality of mixed layer temperature in Arctic shelf seas using a global
simulation that was evaluated against observational and reanalysis products. Through
conducting a mixed layer heat budget analysis, I find that the surface heat flux forcing is
the primary driver of the seasonality change. Third, I investigate recent hydrographic
changes in the Beaufort Gyre region and Bering Strait, using a suite of NEMO simulations
and observational records. The analysis evaluates model performance and identifies
processes responsible for seasonal and interannual freshwater variability in the gyre.
Finally, I examine small mesoscale eddy activity and properties in the western Arctic
Ocean using 2-km resolution observations from the Surface Water and Ocean Topography

(SWOT) mission and model output from a km-scale NEMO model. I reveal three eddy
hotspots in the southern Beaufort Sea and demonstrate that these eddies transport heat and
freshwater from shallow shelves into the deep basin. Together, these findings improve
understanding of the dynamic and thermodynamic processes shaping the polar and
subpolar oceans and contribute to the development of ocean-sea ice models used in
physical oceanography research.

Chapter 1: Introduction
1.1 Oceanic and atmospheric circulation
The Arctic Ocean is an isolated ocean with an area of about 14 million km2,
surrounded by the North American and Eurasian landmasses. The Arctic Ocean
comprises the Canadian and Eurasian Basins with an average depth of approximately
4000 m divided by the Lomonosov Ridge. Arctic shelf seas lie between the deep
basins and the surrounding continental coastline (Timmermans & Marshall, 2020).
The large-scale upper ocean circulation in the Arctic Ocean is characterized by the
Beaufort Gyre (BG) and the Transpolar Drift Stream (Figure 1.1). The Arctic Ocean
receives Atlantic Water (AW) through Fram Strait and the Barents Sea Opening
(BSO) and Pacific Water (PW) via Bering Strait. AW is a typically warm and saline
water mass that originates from the Northern Atlantic Ocean. The Fram Strait Branch
Water is carried by the West Spitsbergen Current (WSC) on the east side of Fram
Strait west of Svalbard (Beszczynska-Möller et al. 2012; Smedsrud et al., 2010). The
Barents Sea Branch Water is conveyed by the upstream Norwegian Atlantic Current
and enters the Barents Sea through two main flows (Smedsrud et al., 2010, 2013).
These two branch waters converge near the St. Anna Trough and form the AW
boundary current that continues to flow along the continental margin of the Eurasian
Basin cyclonically (Figure 1.1). When the boundary current encounters the
1

Lomonosov Ridge, it splits into two branches: one following the ridge and the other
continuing into the Canadian Basin. The pathway of Pacific Water (PW) in the
Chukchi Sea splits into three main routes that follow the seafloor topography (Lin et
al., 2021, 2023; Woodgate, 2018; Woodgate et al., 2015; Muramatsu et al., 2025).
Most of PW reaches and passes through Barrow Canyon and diverges into eastward
and westward components along the Beaufort/Chukchi Sea shelf slope. Another
portion of the PW flows along the northern slope of the Chukchi Sea, known as the
Chukchi Slope Current (Figure 1.1).

atmospheric pressure systems: the anticyclonic Beaufort High centered over the
Canadian Basin and the cyclonic Icelandic Low situated near Iceland in the North
Atlantic Ocean (Timmermans & Marshall, 2020). The geostrophic flows are
constrained by the topographic and bathymetric setting of the Arctic, and the wind
stress curls at the sea surface are modulated by sea ice melting and growth.
Understanding the coupled interactions among the ocean, atmosphere, and sea ice
remains a primary challenge in high-latitude oceanography. The Arctic Ocean, with
its rapid response to climate change, offers a unique natural laboratory for studying
these processes (Timmermans and Marshall, 2020; Fu et al., 2023; Fu & Myers,
2024).
1.2 Arctic sea ice
The sea ice cover in the Arctic Ocean is sensitive to climate change, so its long-term
trend has become one of the most visible representations of global warming observed
since the industrial revolution (Comiso et al., 2017). Arctic sea ice exhibits strong
seasonal variation in extent, with a maximum in March and a minimum in September.
The findings from the satellite observations and sea ice models unanimously have
demonstrated a rapid downward trend of minimum September sea ice cover for the
last several decades (Cavalieri & Parkinson, 2012; Comiso, 2012; Comiso et al., 2017;
Stroeve et al., 2007), and confirm an ongoing acceleration in the decline of the Arctic
4

--- PAGE 33 ---
sea ice cover (Cavalieri & Parkinson, 2012; Stroeve et al., 2007). They also diagnose
a thinning of the average Arctic multiyear ice cover (Comiso, 2012; Spreen et al.,
2020). All these signs indicate a warming Arctic and a possibility of an ice-free Arctic
Ocean (less than 106 km2) in summer, even before 2050 (Guarino et al., 2020). In
contrast, the observed and modeled March trends are much smaller and not significant
(Cavalieri & Parkinson, 2012; Stroeve et al., 2007). The impact of warming is more
likely to be reflected in sea ice thickness than in its extent during the winter months
(Meier et al., 2014; Stroeve & Notz, 2018).
1.3 Warming and freshening of the Arctic Ocean
The Arctic Ocean has been warming at a rate nearly four times the global mean since
1979 and shows the largest temperature change, with substantial influence from the
anthropogenic forcing (Rantanen et al., 2022; Constable et al., 2022). The temperature
change of the Arctic Ocean is principally controlled by the absorption of solar
radiation into the upper ocean, as well as lateral exchanges through gateway straits.
The warming of the Arctic Ocean is tightly interconnected to Arctic sea ice
distribution through the ice-albedo feedback -- a warmer ocean combined with
reduced sea ice cover reflects less sunlight and absorbs more solar energy. This
accelerates the warming of the Arctic Ocean and further amplifies the retreat of Arctic
sea ice, forming a positive feedback loop (Stroeve et al., 2007).

The Arctic Ocean receives a significant amount of freshwater input mainly from the
river runoff having origins in the surrounding North American and Eurasian
landmasses, with other contributions from sea ice melt, PW inflow, and excessive
precipitation minus evaporation (Timmermans & Marshall, 2020). The freshwater is
defined as the zero-salinity water that seawater contains to a referenced salinity of
34.8. The freshwater distribution varies remarkably in the Arctic Ocean, with the
maximum in the Beaufort Sea and the minimum in the southwest Eurasia Basin
(Aagaard & Carmack, 1989). The largest freshwater reservoir in the Beaufort Sea is
closely related to the anticyclonic BG, which plays a crucial role in regulating the
dynamics of the Arctic Ocean (Proshutinsky et al. 2020). The state of the BG is
controlled by the joint effect of three key mechanisms: (i) wind stress forcing, which
drives Ekman transport and convergence (Doddridge et al., 2019; Manucharyan et al.,
2016); (ii) mesoscale eddy fluxes, which counteract freshwater accumulation by
enhancing lateral exchange (Armitage et al., 2020; Meneghello et al., 2017); and (iii)
the ice-ocean governor, a feedback between ice motion and ocean surface currents
that regulates spin-up and stabilization of the gyre (Meneghello et al., 2018, 2020;
Zhong et al., 2018). Changes in the BG’s anticyclonic circulation and associated
freshwater storage may strongly influence the Arctic freshwater budget and the
potential export of low-salinity water to the North Atlantic Ocean, with far-reaching
implications for large-scale climate variability (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2021).

1.4 Labrador Sea and Deep convection
The Labrador Sea, a semi-enclosed basin in the northwestern part of the North
Atlantic, is a subpolar region known to have a direct impact on the processes that
control climate change, where deep convection occurs. Deep convection occurs when
there is a weakly stratified ocean water column beneath the surface mixed layer that
undergoes a great amount of heat loss. The heat loss is attributed to the wintertime
atmospheric conditions, and so its variability is linked to the variation in the North
Atlantic Oscillation (NAO). The direct effect of heat loss is responsible for reducing
the upper ocean layer’s buoyancy, resulting in the preconditioning of the deep
convection. Therefore, the cold and dense surface water sinks into the subsurface
while a net flux of heat and salt is ventilated up to the surface layers. The convective
process leads to the water mixing and thus the formation of the homogeneous
Labrador Sea Water (LSW). The predicted site of the deepest mixed layer in the
southwestern quadrant of the Labrador Sea is the overlapping part between the regions
of maximum buoyancy loss and of weak stratification (Marshall et al., 1998). The
interannual variability of the deepest mixed layer was maximum in 2015 when the
strongest deep convection since the mid-1990s occurred. The intermittent recurrence
of strong convection is predominantly governed by the intensified winter atmospheric
cooling anomaly. It dominates the decadal-scale variability of mid-depth LSW
properties (Yashayaev & Loder, 2016). The deep convection in the Labrador Sea
could be shut down, resulting from the upper layer freshening due to surplus
freshwater export from the Arctic Ocean (Yashayaev, 2024; Zhang et al., 2021).
